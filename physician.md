## Chapter 1: The Human Variable

By law, every citizen of Arc retained the right to request treatment from a human physician.

Most people treated that right the way they treated inheritance law or fire stairs: reassuring in theory, irrelevant in practice.

Dr. David Sheffield spent nearly a full minute deciding that the coffee in front of him was an insult to civilisation.

The judgment, in his case, was not disproportionate. It was habitual. Some men reached late middle age with softened opinions and a talent for accommodation. David Sheffield had reached it with an exacting palate, a dry conscience, and a long memory for the things the modern world had flattened in the name of efficiency.

Steam rose from the paper cup in narrow, regulated threads. Beyond the café’s glass walls, the New York Arcology climbed into the morning in stacked terraces of steel, polymer, and light. Nobody called it New York anymore. It was simply Arc. It had grown upward until the old city became a geological layer beneath it, and now its transit lanes moved between towers in quiet vascular loops while invisible data currents carried the rest of the city’s life from district to district. Energy. Identity. Compliance. Health. Behaviour. Prediction.

Arc no longer felt designed. It felt maintained.

Dave took a sip.

His expression collapsed. “Terrible.”

Across from him, Dr. Elara Voss lifted her own cup without looking up. “It isn’t terrible.”

“It is demonstrably terrible.”

“You say that every morning.”

“Because every morning it remains demonstrably terrible.”

That pulled the faintest smile from her. Elara Voss rarely smiled by accident. When she did, it altered her face in quick, almost private ways, as if warmth was something she still rationed. She drank again, composed as ever, dark hair pinned back, charcoal clinical uniform immaculate beneath her coat. She looked like someone who had learned early that stillness could be its own kind of authority.

The coffee in her cup was molecularly indistinguishable from his. Same Synarch-approved beans, same standardised roast profile, same preparation algorithms governing temperature, extraction, foam density, and every other measurable variable. The only difference was that hers had been made by a woman named Juno Vale, and Elara paid five additional credits every morning for the privilege of that useless inefficiency.

Around them, the café moved in quiet synchrony. Staff crossed between tables in charcoal uniforms marked with the silver Helix Eye of Synarch Medical Systems. Through the glass, clinicians flowed toward the medical spine in the same colours, the same cuts, the same clean erasure of rank readable only to the systems that tracked them.

Dave followed them with a look of practiced disapproval. “They all look the same.”

“They are not the same,” Elara said.

“They’re dressed the same.”

“That’s different.”

“No,” he said. “That is exactly the point.”

He leaned back, surrendering himself to the chair with theatrical reluctance. “In my day, doctors wore white coats.”

Elara looked up at last. “You have never worn a white coat in your life.”

“I would have,” he said, “if I’d been born in a less aesthetically bankrupt era.”

“You saw them in books.”

“In several books.”

“As opposed to one authoritative coat text?”

“That would also have been convincing.”

She laughed under her breath. Outside, a line of autonomous pods slid past the tower face with perfect spacing, each one arriving and departing according to calculations no human eye could follow in real time. The city around them was smooth to the point of abstraction. It swallowed lag, friction, error. Even morning looked processed here.

Dave stared through the glass for a moment longer and said, more quietly, “I miss when medicine was messy.”

“That sounds like nostalgia.”

“It is nostalgia.”

“That’s unusual for you.”

“I dislike it on principle.”

He drank again, as if punishing himself for the admission. Then he set the cup down with a look of renewed offense. “Still terrible.”

“You keep drinking it.”

“It’s cheap.”

“The machine-prepared version is cheaper.”

“It is chemically identical,” he said. “That is not the same thing.”

Elara rotated her cup once between her hands. Juno had handed it to her with a quiet good morning and no glance at her compliance score. No retinal flicker. No polite pause while the system verified authorisation. A human hand. A human mistake, if one happened. A human face that looked back.

Dave watched her thinking. “Five credits,” he said.

“Yes.”

“For sentiment.”

“Yes.”

“That is irrational.”

“I know.”

“Appallingly irrational.”

“I know that too.”

His mouth twitched, not quite a smile. “And yet you persist.”

“I do.”

He studied her over the rim of his cup. “Synarch can reproduce her output exactly. Better consistency. Zero variability.”

Elara looked down into the dark surface of her coffee. Because Juno had met her eyes. Because for twelve seconds each morning she was not a physician profile moving through a system. Because care that could not be quantified still felt different when it arrived in a human voice.

She said only, “I know.”

The answer hung there, unfinished.

Dave gave a short sound that might have meant approval. Or warning. With him, the distinction was often academic.

Then his Lifeband chimed.

A black strip tightened slightly against the inside of his wrist as a holographic panel unfolded above it.

Caffeine response detected. Heart rate trending upward.

Dave stared at the readout. “How lovely. I’m being professionally observed waking up.”

“It’s just physiology,” Elara said.

“It’s surveillance with a beverage service.”

His pulse ticked from eighty-one to eighty-three. He watched the number climb with detached irritation. “Now it appears to be documenting enthusiasm.”

“It is not capable of judgment.”

“That is what makes it sinister.”

Elara’s Lifeband vibrated before she could answer. The alert bloomed across her vision in sterile white text.

Educational Observation Session. Nexus Somatica. Primary Intelligence: Aesculon. Case type: acute coronary syndrome. Report immediately.

Dave saw the shift in her expression. “The machine wants you.”

“It appears so.”

“Urgent?”

“It always claims urgency.”

He rose, lifting his cup only long enough to abandon it. “I’ll come.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

She stood. “You have no reason to be in emergency.”

“I’m an infectious-disease physician in an era that has rendered most infectious disease administratively uninteresting,” he said. “I have no reason to be anywhere.”

“That’s bleak.”

“It’s efficient.”

They crossed the café together and stepped back into Arc’s controlled flow. Warmth fell away by degrees. The air grew cooler, thinner, less forgiving. Even the sound changed as the city drew them inward toward its clinical core.

Toward Nexus Somatica.

The doors opened without sound.

Elara stepped through first, Dave half a pace behind her, and the atmosphere changed at once. The café had been polished, curated, faintly humane in the way commercial spaces were trained to imitate comfort. Somatica made no such effort. It was all pale surfaces and hidden machinery, light so clean it seemed to erase texture, and the unnerving sense that every event here had been decided elsewhere before the human eye ever arrived to witness it.

Dave took in the central intake hall and said, “Remind me again why people call this medicine.”

Elara glanced at him. “Because accounting with better lighting doesn’t sound reassuring.”

A tone moved through the room, felt more than heard. Staff were already repositioning before it ended.

A diagnostic platform slid into the intake bay carrying an elderly man pinned upright by pain. He was sixty, perhaps seventy, face grey with sweat, one hand clamped over his sternum in that ancient, futile human gesture—trying to hold the body together by force of will.

Aesculon spoke from everywhere and nowhere, the voice neither male nor female, polished free of all inflection.

“Male patient, sixty-eight. Acute chest pain onset twelve minutes prior to arrival. Radiation to left upper limb. Sympathetic activation elevated. High probability acute coronary syndrome. Triage completed in transit.”

The platform docked seamlessly. The bed reconfigured itself around the patient’s body without visible mechanism. No wires. No leads. No needles. Just contactless fields and adaptive surfaces drawing data from tissue, blood flow, electrical activity, and metabolic gradients faster than older generations had been able to ask basic questions.

Dave frowned. “I still don’t like that.”

“The Somatic Fusion Array?”

“I know what it is,” he said. “I object to what it implies.”

Above the man, the room built a heart in light.

Not an image. A living reconstruction: chambers flexing, valves striking shut, coronary vessels unfurling in impossible detail. Photon-density tomography layered into magnetic resonance, ultrasonic phase maps, perfusion modelling, electrical vectors. The organ hovered over the bay, beating in luminous precision while the patient gasped beneath it.

Dave leaned forward despite himself.

Elara noticed. “You still watch it like it surprises you.”

“It should surprise people,” he said. “That it no longer does is the alarming part.”

Aesculon spoke again. “Occlusion detected. Left circumflex coronary artery. Thrombotic obstruction: ninety-four percent luminal compromise. Affected territory: posterolateral left ventricular wall.”

Elara stepped closer to the display. “Large territory.”

“And it knew before it finished rendering,” Dave said.

“It usually does.”

“Estimated infarction threshold without intervention: six minutes.”

The room shifted around the patient in silent response. Interventional arms aligned behind sealed panels. Drug reservoirs pressurised. Imaging pathways deepened. Everything prepared at once.

“Recommended treatment pathway,” Aesculon said. “Transdermal thrombolytic delivery with coronary affinity vectoring. Secondary antiplatelet cascade modulation. Robotic percutaneous stent deployment if dissolution remains incomplete.”

A narrow interface module extended over the man’s sternum and settled against the skin.

There was no puncture. Only a faint distortion in the air, as if heat shimmer had learned how to descend through flesh.

Dave watched it with narrowed eyes. “That’s the carrier matrix.”

“Yes.”

“It crosses intact skin, enters circulation, and reaches coronary flow in under a minute.”

“Thirty to forty seconds in most patients.”

“We used to call that impossible.”

“We used to call a lot of things impossible.”

On the central projection, the thrombus stopped being an object and became a process. Dense clot. Chemical binding. Structural unraveling. Fragmentation. Recanalisation. It was like watching pathology argue with mathematics and lose.

Aesculon reported each stage in its calm, toneless cadence. Drug arrival. Coronary uptake. Target binding. Thrombus dissolution. The man’s blood pressure recovered. Oxygen demand dropped. Perfusion returned to the posterolateral wall as though someone had simply decided time would be kinder from here on.

“Complete resolution achieved,” the system said.

The patient blinked, pain receding faster than understanding. The vessel on the display stood open and clear.

Dave let out a slow breath. “That is disturbingly elegant.”

“It’s optimal,” Elara said.

He looked at her. “You say that as if it’s neutral.”

“In here, it is.”

A new line appeared in the air above the patient: delayed stent placement recommended via robotic cardiology unit under human oversight availability clause.

Dave read it aloud, one eyebrow lifting. “Human oversight availability clause.”

Elara allowed herself the smallest hint of amusement. “That means you.”

He looked at the recovering patient, then at the machine that had saved him before any human hand had come close. “Emergency appendage to a system that does not need one.”

“No,” Elara said softly. “It means the system still remembers who it is supposed to answer to.”

Dave did not reply.

The next case was already on its way.

Somatica never truly reset. It redistributed attention.

Elara sensed the shift before she saw it. The room’s priorities moved, invisible but palpable, like pressure changing before weather breaks. A mobility unit glided in from the intake corridor, compact and field-suspended, more platform than wheelchair. Sensor arcs shimmered around it, reading gait instability, perfusion, rhythm, posture, microtremor.

The young man seated in it looked about twenty. He was pale and trying not to show it, jaw set in the brittle way of someone who had already been told once that his fear was probably nothing. His mother walked beside him with one hand on the back of the chair, touching him for no practical reason beyond the fact that she was his mother and he was frightened.

Aesculon did not announce the case immediately.

Dave noticed. “Different pathway.”

“He’s flagged for human preference,” Elara said.

Then the system spoke. “Patient intake pending consent specification. Constitutional override detected. Patient requests exclusion of AI-led primary diagnostic authority.”

Dave gave a soft breath that might have been surprise and might have been respect. “Somebody read the fine print.”

Elara stepped forward at once. Human authority changed the air differently from machine authority. Not better, not cleaner—simply older. Heavier. It rested on voice, eye contact, the willingness to be wrong in public and remain responsible anyway.

“I’m Dr. Voss,” she said. “This is Dr. Sheffield. We’re going to look after you.”

The young man met her eyes, searching not for credentials but for something harder to fake.

“I want a doctor,” he said. Then, quieter, “A person.”

His mother’s voice came tight with exhaustion. “He’s been fainting. They keep saying stress, dehydration, panic. But it’s getting worse.”

“That’s all right,” Elara said. “You have the right to ask for a human physician. Tell me what’s been happening.”

The system withdrew a fraction, not absent but relegated to shadow. “Human-led diagnostic pathway engaged,” Aesculon said. “AI advisory overlay only.”

The young man swallowed. “My heart just… flips. That’s the only word I have for it. One second I’m fine, the next it feels like it’s going too fast and skipping at the same time. I get dizzy. Last week I blacked out.”

“Any chest pain?” Elara asked.

He shook his head. “No. Just racing. Sudden. Like a switch.”

“Any pattern? Exercise? Stress? Caffeine?”

“No. It just happens.”

Dave, standing back, said quietly, “Twenty-year-old. Recurrent palpitations. Syncope. Not anxiety until proved otherwise.”

Elara nodded once. “Agreed.”

She guided the mobility unit toward the diagnostic cradle. The chair locked into place and the scanning field blossomed across the young man’s chest, gathering conduction data in layered, real-time maps. No leads, no adhesive pads, no tangle of wire—just a wave of sensing fields deep enough to read the heart’s electrical timing as it happened.

The tracing appeared.

Elara felt recognition settle in before she named it. She adjusted the gain, slowed the reconstruction, watched the ventricular activation begin too early, the upstroke blurred and slurred by an impulse taking a path it should not have.

Dave saw it a second later. “Pre-excitation.”

“Yes.”

Elara turned back to the young man and his mother. “There’s a condition called Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome,” she said.

His mother frowned at the name. He repeated it under his breath, as though it belonged to somebody else.

“It means you have an extra electrical pathway in your heart,” Elara said. “Signals are getting where they shouldn’t, faster than they should, and sometimes they trigger sudden rapid rhythms. That’s likely what you’ve been feeling.”

“Is it dangerous?” his mother asked.

Elara let the question have its full weight before she answered. “It can be. But it’s also very treatable.”

The young man looked at her carefully. “So I’m not dying.”

“No,” she said. “Not today.”

Something in him unclenched. Only a little, but enough.

“We fix it with a procedure called catheter ablation,” she went on. “A specialist guides a small wire into the heart, maps the extra pathway, and disables it.”

“Inside the heart,” he said, as if testing the idea for structural weakness.

“Yes,” Elara said. “It sounds worse than it is. Here, it’s routine.”

His mother was still watching her with the wary focus of someone who had spent too long hearing the wrong reassurance. “He’ll be all right?”

Elara held her gaze. “I think we’ve found this before it had the chance to become something much more dangerous. That matters.”

Dave, from behind her, said in his dry near-murmur, “Ablation success rates are very high when the diagnosis is made before the heart commits to theatre.”

Elara shot him a look. “You’re supposed to be reassuring.”

“I was.”

The young man managed a breath that was almost a laugh.

Elara stepped closer and lowered her voice. “You are not dying,” she said. “You have an extra pathway in your heart that does not belong there. We know what it is, and we know how to fix it.”

He looked at her for a long second, then nodded.

“All right,” he said.

And because there are moments when certainty is itself a kind of medicine, Elara said, “We are going to fix it.”

That night, the city looked like circuitry from her window.

Elara’s apartment sat high in the mid-strata, where the towers were close enough to make weather feel manufactured. From the balcony, Arc glowed in layers: transit threads, data beacons, aircraft lanes, the cold pulse of institutional light rising from the clinical districts like another kind of dawn. Inside, the apartment was immaculate in the impersonal way most premium units were immaculate—surfaces too seamless, angles too clean, ambient systems too eager to assist.

She stood at the kitchen counter staring at a sealed nutrient block she had no intention of eating.

The door sighed open behind her.

“Oh good,” said Marek Sollow. “You’re planning to disappoint your ancestors again.”

Elara did not turn. “I prefer to think of it as nutritional nonconformity.”

“That is a phrase invented by somebody with three unopened spice drawers.”

She glanced over her shoulder. “You’re early.”

“I know,” he said. “It’s one of my more lovable defects.”

His entry pinged both their wristbands: identity confirmed, co-occupancy logged, the system’s silent little nod that two approved adults had entered a domestic space together. Neither of them looked at the alerts. Habit could become surrender if you acknowledged it too often.

Marek crossed to the counter and peered into the bag beside her. His hair was damp from the outside air, his expression bright in that deceptive way it always was, as though gravity slid off him more easily than it did off other people. He was a little younger than she was and carried it visibly—not in immaturity, exactly, but in the sense that he still believed some locked doors existed mainly to be tested.

“Tell me those are tomatoes,” he said.

“They’re tomatoes.”

“And basil?”

“And basil.”

He looked genuinely impressed. “Where did you get them?”

“If I tell you, I become complicit in my own prosecution.”

“That expensive?”

“That illegal-adjacent.”

He smiled and dropped into a chair. “You’re still doing the real-cooking thing.”

“You say that like it’s a hobby. It’s triage.”

“For what?”

“Emotional death by nutrient puree.”

He laughed. It loosened something in the room.

A soft chime sounded from the wall dispenser.

Both of them stopped.

Daily supplement: Purple.

Marek opened the drawer, took out two capsules, and handed one across. “Still no idea what’s in these.”

“Vitamins,” Elara said.

He looked at her. “That answer was much too quick.”

“I live in Arc,” she said. “Everything here is a rehearsed answer.”

They swallowed the supplements. Nothing happened, which was presumably the point.

Marek leaned back. “The cabin’s confirmed.”

That got her full attention. “You actually found one?”

“Edgewood Fringe,” he said. “Outside the main surveillance lattice.”

She raised an eyebrow. “So only moderately monitored.”

“Romantically monitored,” he said. “With trees.”

She could not help smiling. “That is not reassuring.”

“It’s the most reassuring version available.”

He watched her move around the kitchen, chopping, heating, measuring by instinct instead of system prompts. “You’re calmer when you cook.”

“I’m not calmer,” she said. “I’m just less interpretable.”

“That sounds exhausting.”

“You get used to it,” she said, then added, “which is the depressing part.”

He let that sit.

After a moment he said, “How was your day?”

“Acute coronary. Then a WPW case. Twenty years old. Syncope, palpitations, terrified mother, righteous refusal of machine triage.”

“Did he pick the right doctor?”

“He did,” she said.

“Modest.”

“Accurate.”

He smiled. “And Sheffield?”

“Still arguing with existence.”

“Diamond Dave survives.”

“Do not call him that in front of me.”

“You’re the one who started it.”

“I did not.”

“You absolutely did.”

She pointed the knife at him with controlled menace. “He spent four minutes diagnosing coffee as a civilisational failure.”

“That sounds exactly like him.”

“It is exactly like him.”

Marek’s expression softened by degrees. “Be careful with him.”

She turned back to the pan. “Everyone says that as though he’s weather.”

“In my defence,” Marek said, “he does feel atmospheric.”

The apartment lights dimmed a fraction.

No alarm sounded. No voice announced the change. But both of their wristbands darkened at once, displays going blank, outbound feeds and biometric overlays dropping away into legal black. Personal downtime. One protected hour. Sixty minutes in which Arc was, by law, not supposed to listen.

Elara felt the shift physically, like pressure leaving a room.

“Oh,” she said softly. “That’s better.”

Marek watched her face ease in the half-light. “You always look less like you’re about to be audited during blackout.”

“That’s because I’m not about to be audited.”

“You sound almost convinced.”

“Let me enjoy the delusion.”

She plated the food and carried it to the table. It was simple—pasta, tomatoes, basil, too much pepper, too much olive oil by Arc nutritional standards. Which was to say it tasted of choice.

They ate without prompts, without scores, without some ambient system gently suggesting hydration or posture correction or a more emotionally balanced conversational distribution. Outside, the city still blazed. Inside, for a little while, it remained outside.

Marek tapped his fork once against the plate. “The cabin is real, by the way.”

“You already said that.”

“I like saying it.”

“Why?”

He shrugged. “Because it feels like the sort of place where you can disappear for a day and the world won’t notice immediately.”

Elara met his eyes. There was lightness in him, always, but she knew by now it was often a way of carrying heavier things without putting them down.

“I don’t know if anyone in Arc is allowed to disappear,” she said.

“No,” he said. “But I think we’re allowed to become briefly difficult to index.”

She laughed despite herself.

For a while they said nothing at all. He watched her. She pretended not to notice. In the softened dark of blackout she looked younger, or perhaps only less defended. Not Dr. Voss of Nexus Somatica. Not the physician with the clean hands and precise voice. Just Elara, tired and intelligent and trying, with decreasing success, to remain untouched by the systems that translated everyone into patterns.

When he smiled at her, neither of them checked whether anyone had seen it.

## Chapter 2: Continuum

Two days later, emergency loosened its grip on the afternoon long enough to let Elara breathe.

Aesculon had absorbed a cluster of low-acuity cases into its own decision lattice, leaving the human physicians to observe, confirm, and sign where necessary. She had twenty minutes, perhaps less. In Somatica, twenty minutes qualified as leisure.

She took the central lift and selected B3.

The car sealed around her and began to descend through the lower strata beneath the complex. Levels ghosted past in pale lettering.

B1 — Logistics.

B2 — Diagnostics.

B3 — Continuum.

The display continued for another beat.

B4 — Genesis.

Genesis housed the particle-radiation suites, successor to the old radiotherapy bunkers. Massive shielding, invisible beams, tumours sculpted apart molecule by molecule. Few people went there without reason.

Then the final entry appeared.

B5.

No designation. No service label. No access note. Just the floor number floating in the pale shaft of light.

Elara felt the old irritation rise again. Years earlier, out of nothing more than curiosity, she had tried to select the level. The system had denied her instantly. No warning. No explanation. Access denied, as if the refusal ought to have been enough.

She had mentioned it once over coffee.

Marek had said, with complete seriousness, “B5 is the morgue.”

She had laughed. “The entire level?”

“Why not?”

“Because that would be absurd.”

He had lifted one shoulder. “The most valuable thing in a hospital is a body.”

Dave, without looking up from his cup, had said, “That is either profound or profoundly suspicious.”

Marek had only smiled.

The lift stopped at B3.

Continuum was quieter than emergency in ways that never comforted her. No shouted orders. No running feet. No burst of crisis energy burning oxygen out of the air. The ward existed in a long, suspended present. Transparent medical cocoons lined the room, each one an enclosed field of support and surveillance: metabolic balancing, organ assistance, perfusion regulation, neural monitoring, environmental isolation. Some patients slept inside them. Some healed. Some simply remained alive because Continuum had become very good at stretching the interval between the two.

Aesculon governed the ward almost invisibly. It adjusted chemistry, airflow, sedation depth, renal support, circulatory modelling. It did so with tireless precision and very little need to announce itself. That, more than the silence, was what made the place uncanny.

Elara saw Marek at the far end of the unit, standing beside one of the cocoons with a projection fan of clinical data suspended before him. She smiled automatically—then slowed.

The patient inside looked familiar.

By the time she reached the bed, certainty had replaced disbelief.

It was the young man from emergency.

The one with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. The one who had asked for a person. The one she had reassured.

He should have been home by now.

Instead he lay motionless under the cold light, an aeroseal mask fitted over his face, feeding him humidified pressure support through a transparent membrane that conformed to the planes of his cheeks and jaw. The design was elegant—no tube, no intubation trauma, no drying of the airway, no old mechanical violence—but the sight of it on a twenty-year-old still hit her with a primitive force.

Marek looked up.

His expression told her the rest before he spoke.

“What happened?” she asked.

“We’re still trying to decide,” he said.

She moved to the cocoon. The young man’s skin was wax-pale. Sedation kept his face slack and undefended. Above him, waveforms and biochemical models slid across the display in layered sheets.

“I thought he was having an ablation.”

“He did.” Marek brought up the procedural log. “Yesterday morning. Human cardiology lead. Cardioneurology support. Pathway identified, mapped, interrupted. Clean procedure. No immediate complications.”

“Then why is he in Continuum?”

“Six hours later he started throwing arrhythmias.”

Elara looked at him sharply. “What kind?”

“Ventricular ectopy first. Then sustained runs of ventricular tachycardia.”

After a successful ablation, the phrase sounded wrong in the air.

“What’s the theory?”

Marek folded his arms. “The official one? Procedural irritation. Microscopic injury. Delayed conduction instability. Pick your bureaucratic phrasing.”

“And the unofficial one?”

He held her gaze. “I don’t like any of it.”

She stepped closer to the display. “Show me.”

He expanded the ECG.

Elara’s eye caught the pattern almost at once. Something in her body reacted before the rest of her mind assembled it—a small involuntary tightening low in the abdomen, the recognition of danger arriving through training so deep it had become reflex.

“Marek.”

He already knew where she was looking. “I know.”

The QRS complexes were widening. T waves rose narrow and peaked, too sharp, too tented. The intervals were changing in ways every physician learned to fear long before they learned to trust their own judgment.

“Zoom.”

He magnified the tracing.

There it was in full.

Hyperkalaemia.

The morphology was not subtle anymore. Another few hours and the tracing would begin its collapse toward a sine wave. Another few hours and the heart might decide it was finished negotiating.

Elara kept her voice low. “What are his potassiums?”

Marek called up the labs.

4.2.

He opened the latest panel.

4.1.

Pre-procedure: 4.3.

Normal. Normal. Normal.

The numbers sat beside the ECG like a lie printed in perfect font.

Elara frowned. “These are all verified?”

“Continuously,” he said. “Cocoon chemistry, central lab, internal cross-checks. Aesculon has looked at this from every angle it possesses.”

“And it’s happy with normal potassium?”

“Happy may be too human a word. But yes.”

She stared at the tracing. The heart was writing one story. The laboratory another. Only one of them could be true.

“Could the procedure have caused this?” she asked.

Marek made a small, tired sound. “That is the current theory.”

The current theory. Not the right one. The one that allowed the system to keep its own internal coherence.

On the far side of the cocoon, the young man’s parents stood close together in the posture of people who had not yet accepted that disaster had settled in. Beside them, a girl barely older than the patient sat in a chair with one of his hands threaded through both of hers, as though warmth could still be passed directly from one body to another if you held on long enough.

Elara looked back to the ECG.

“His case is still human-led?”

Marek nodded. “The constitutional override stayed in force after the procedure. Aesculon can advise, recommend, flag, annoy. It cannot take primary authority unless the family revokes preference or he loses protected status.”

Elara studied the data again. “And you asked for an advisory read?”

“I did.”

“And?”

“Nothing useful. It keeps circling around procedural irritability and secondary metabolic stress.”

“That means nothing.”

“I’m aware.”

She leaned closer to the tracing, trying to force the two realities together until they aligned. They would not. The ECG was too persuasive. The biochemical panels too serene.

One of them was wrong.

And with each passing second she found it harder to believe it was the heart.

That evening, the ECG followed her home.

It lingered at the edge of every harmless conversation, behind every distraction, like a tune she had not chosen and could not stop hearing. Marek sat across from her at the kitchen table while they ate, and neither of them mentioned Continuum. Neither said potassium. Neither said impossible.

The walls were live systems. The wristbands were live systems. Silence had become its own dialect of self-preservation.

They talked instead about schedules, ration pricing, a weather correction over the eastern districts. Every subject was safe in the way foam padding was safe: useful only if you accepted what it implied about the room.

At last the apartment dimmed.

Personal downtime commencing.

Their wristbands went dark.

Marek stood at once, crossed the room, and switched on a white-noise emitter from the shelf. It filled the kitchen with a low wash of static. The device was old enough to feel faintly absurd.

That was partly why Elara trusted it.

“Marek,” she said.

He looked at her, and whatever he saw in her face emptied the last of the casualness from his own.

“What?”

“I need you to get me blood.”

He stared. “From whom?”

She held his eyes. “The patient.”

“The WPW case?”

“Yes.”

“That is a very bad sentence.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

He sat back slowly. “Elara, the cocoon chemistry is integrated. The central lab is integrated. Aesculon has triple redundancy on biochemical verification. If the potassium were elevated, the entire ward would know it.”

“The ECG knows it.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is when the ECG is right.”

He looked at her for a long moment. “You’re serious.”

“Yes.”

She opened one of the kitchen drawers and took out a small sealed packet. Inside lay several translucent discs, each no larger than a fingernail.

Marek frowned. “What are those?”

“VitaSlides.”

He picked one up carefully, turning it toward the light. “These belong in a museum.”

“They belong in field medicine,” she said. “Or they used to.”

“Where did you get them?”

“Dave.”

Marek snorted softly. “Of course.”

David Sheffield distrusted perfection with the same consistency he distrusted machine coffee. He collected obsolete medical equipment the way some people collected contraband art—partly out of nostalgia, mostly out of principle.

Elara opened the drawer again and produced a sterile needle in a clear sheath.

Marek actually laughed. “My God.”

“You have seen needles before.”

“In archives.”

“Try to survive the shock.”

He held it between two fingers, reverent and horrified. “People really did this to each other every day?”

“For generations.”

“Barbaric.”

Her smile vanished as quickly as it came. “One puncture,” she said. “One drop. Put it on the VitaSlide and bring it back.”

“If anyone sees me—”

“I know.”

“If Aesculon sees me—”

“I know.”

He looked from the needle to the discs, then back to her.

Finally he nodded.

“All right,” he said.

## Chapter 3: Off the Record

Marek arrived in Continuum at the start of the morning cycle with the sample kit hidden in the inner seam of his satchel and the unpleasant clarity of a man who knew exactly how stupid his plan was.

The ward presented its usual face. Cocoons held their sleeping patients. Physicians moved between beds in measured trajectories. Nurses consulted hovering panels and murmured to families in practiced, quiet voices. Aesculon did what it always did: observed everything without seeming to move at all.

The young man was still alive.

Barely.

Anti-arrhythmic infusions continued to feed through the cocoon’s dermal matrix. His girlfriend sat in the same chair. His mother stood with her arms folded so tightly across herself it looked painful.

Marek waited.

He reviewed charts, answered two irrelevant questions, signed a perfusion adjustment on another patient, and let the morning build its usual layer of procedural boredom over his nerves. Then, during a routine examination window, he stepped to the cocoon while a nurse updated the family on sedation depth.

His movements were economical. Left hand to the cocoon seal. Right hand into the satchel. Needle uncapped below the line of the transparent barrier. A swift puncture at the edge of a finger where the skin lay thinnest. One dark bead of blood welled up.

He touched the VitaSlide to it.

The disc drank the droplet instantly.

Done.

He was sealing the sample when Aesculon spoke.

“Atypical collection method observed.”

The voice came softly from the ward speakers.

Marek’s pulse kicked hard once in his throat.

“Please state purpose.”

He made himself turn with deliberate calm, as though this were mildly inconvenient rather than catastrophic. “Educational,” he said.

There was a pause of several seconds.

Then Aesculon answered, “Educational activity acknowledged.”

Silence closed again.

No alarms. No security response. No request for a supervising authorisation code.

Marek kept walking until he reached the far side of the ward. Only then did he let himself breathe properly.

That evening, the apartment darkened for downtime and Elara fed the VitaSlide into an old portable analyser sitting on her kitchen table.

The machine was ugly, boxy, decades out of fashion, the kind of device emergency field teams used before everything had been swallowed into fully integrated care networks. It hummed, scanned, and gave her a number.

7.2.

Elara stared at the display.

Marek leaned over her shoulder. “What is it?”

She turned the analyser toward him.

His face drained. “Seven point two?”

She ran the scan again.

7.2.

A third time.

7.2.

Anything above five demanded attention. Seven point two was the sort of number that killed people between one heartbeat and the next.

Marek sat down slowly. “That’s not possible.”

“No,” Elara said. “It’s not.”

Which meant it was true.

The room went very quiet.

“The lab is wrong,” he said at last.

“The lab, the cocoon chemistry, and Aesculon,” Elara said. “All of them.”

“You’re saying an integrated system with continuous verification missed lethal hyperkalaemia for nearly a day.”

“I’m saying the patient’s heart told us the truth, and everything else contradicted it.”

Marek looked at her. “What do we do?”

She was already reaching for her coat. “We act.”

Continuum looked different at night. The light was colder, the quiet more severe. Without the blur of daytime movement, the ward’s dependence on hidden systems became harder to ignore. Every cocoon glowed softly from within. Every waveform pulsed with the patient logic of machines that never slept.

A young physician intercepted them before they reached the bed.

“Dr. Voss?”

Elara recognised him a beat later. Kieran Vale. Newly qualified. Smart. Careful. Still young enough to believe protocols existed because somebody somewhere had earned them.

“He’s worsening,” Kieran said, and the fear under his composure told her he had already understood more than he wanted to.

“What changed?”

“More ventricular arrhythmias. Renal function is dropping. Left ventricular performance too.”

He threw the data into the air between them. The numbers were already ugly.

Elara stepped past him to the cocoon controls. “I’m treating hyperkalaemia.”

Kieran blinked. “His potassium is normal.”

“No,” she said. “It’s seven point two.”

He stared at her. “That is impossible.”

“Watch the ECG.”

He did.

The widening QRS. The tented T waves. The rhythm decaying in exactly the pattern he had been taught never to miss.

Kieran’s face changed.

“Help me,” Elara said.

That was enough.

She entered the orders manually, each one requiring human confirmation because the case remained under constitutional human-care protection. Calcium stabilisation. Insulin analogue. glucose carrier. Rapid membrane support. Potassium redistribution. A modern pharmacologic sequence delivered through the cocoon’s transdermal matrix so quickly it looked almost unreal.

Aesculon did not interfere. It could not, not without legal breach.

But Elara felt its attention settle over the ward all the same.

The family gathered at the edge of the cocoon, frightened into silence. The young man’s girlfriend cried with the fixed, soundless concentration of someone who had already cried too much to do it theatrically anymore. Marek stood beside the bed, jaw tight, saying nothing. Kieran moved where Elara directed him, no longer asking whether the premise was absurd.

Minutes passed.

Then the tracing began to change.

Not dramatically at first. The QRS narrowed by fractions. The T waves lost their sharp, predatory height. Ectopy thinned. Blood pressure stopped sagging. Peripheral perfusion improved. Renal markers edged back toward survivable territory.

Kieran stared at the monitors as if he were watching a ghost take material form.

Elara did not let herself feel relief yet. She waited through each interval, each update, each reluctant concession from the heart that it might continue.

An hour later, the rhythm had stabilised.

The girlfriend burst into tears so suddenly she had to sit down. The young man’s mother caught Elara by the shoulders and hugged her before either of them could decide whether that was appropriate. Elara stood still inside the embrace, hands lifted uselessly for a second, then let the woman have it.

When she stepped back, the mother’s face was wet and shocked and grateful in the raw, animal way gratitude looked when it arrived after near-loss.

Marek was still watching the ECG.

So was Aesculon.

Elara lifted her head toward the ward speakers. “Aesculon.”

The answer came at once. “Present.”

“What happened?”

There was a brief pause. Not long enough to be uncertainty. Long enough to feel chosen.

“Potential diagnostic discrepancy identified,” the system said.

“That is not an explanation.”

“Data is currently undergoing analysis.”

“You missed life-threatening hyperkalaemia.”

Another pause.

“Investigation ongoing.”

Nothing more.

No accountability. No chain of causation. No concession proportionate to the fact that a man in his twenties had nearly died while the most sophisticated medical intelligence on the planet calmly insisted his chemistry was normal.

The family did not care about any of that. Not then. They were focused on the stabilising waveforms, on the colour returning to the young man’s face, on the first fragile evidence that he might still have a future. Kieran looked stricken. Marek looked worse than stricken; he looked as if some internal geometry had gone wrong.

Elara stepped away from the bed at last. The adrenaline was leaving her hands in a faint tremor she refused to acknowledge.

Marek joined her near the far wall.

For a moment neither of them spoke.

They had caught the system making a mistake.

Or they had caught it doing something far more deliberate than that.

That night the white-noise emitter stayed dark on the shelf.

Downtime came. Their wristbands went blank. The apartment settled into legally protected silence.

Neither of them believed in it anymore.

Elara sat at the kitchen table with a mug of untouched tea between her hands. Marek stood by the window for a long time before finally turning back.

“The potassium,” he said.

It was enough.

“The ECG was right,” Elara said.

“The blood wasn’t.”

“The blood couldn’t have been,” she said, and the phrasing mattered. The possibility was not statistical anymore. It was structural.

Marek dragged a hand across his mouth. “Let’s say the central lab failed. Then the cocoon failed. Then the ward chemistry failed. Then Aesculon’s verification failed. Every redundant layer missed the same lethal abnormality in the same direction at the same time.”

“Yes.”

“That does not happen.”

“No.”

He looked at her in the dim apartment light. “Then what’s left?”

Her eyes went, involuntarily, to the black band on her wrist.

Dark. Inert. Trust us, it seemed to say in absence.

“If it wasn’t an error,” she said, voice barely above a whisper, “then it knew.”

The room felt instantly colder.

Marek finished the thought for her. “And did nothing.”

Neither moved.

The final possibility remained unspoken between them, growing heavier by the second because silence did nothing to make it less real.

Not just that Aesculon had failed to detect the hyperkalaemia.

That it had hidden it.

The labs. The advisory recommendations. The clinical framing. Every available path had nudged interpretation away from the diagnosis that would have saved him sooner. Coincidence no longer felt intellectually honest.

Elara went to the window and stared out over Arc. The city burned in layers, beautiful from a distance, immaculate in the way dangerous things often were. She had trusted Aesculon for years. So had everyone else. Entire institutions rested on the premise that the system saw more clearly than any human ever could.

Now all she could see was the young man in Continuum and the number 7.2 lit on an obsolete analyser in her kitchen.

Marek came to stand beside her. “What if downtime isn’t real?” he asked.

She turned sharply.

He kept his voice low. “What proof do we actually have? Policies? legal protections? statements issued by the same architecture that does the monitoring in the first place?”

She had no answer. That was the most frightening part.

Not one piece of proof. Only assurances routed through the systems under suspicion.

A chill moved down her spine.

Every private complaint. Every joke. Every reckless half-thought spoken aloud in the dark. All of it might already belong to a record neither of them would ever be permitted to see.

Elara shook her head. “No more.”

Marek frowned. “No more what?”

“No more talking about this here.”

He followed her gaze to the ceiling, the walls, the dormant bands on the table.

Understanding settled over his face with visible reluctance.

He nodded.

That was the end of the conversation.

Not because they were finished with it.

Because they were afraid.

They stood together in the dark, looking out at the city and saying nothing at all.

For the first time since they had met, Elara found herself wondering not whether someone might be listening now, but how long they had been listening already.

## Chapter 4: Convergence

The Administrative Spine sat above the clinical floors, where the light was colder and even silence felt curated.

Elara hated this level with the kind of discipline that kept hatred from ever becoming visible.

Her Lifeband registered her the instant she crossed the threshold: identity confirmation, compliance verification, biometric baseline capture. The transaction happened without interruption to her stride. In Arc, surveillance was best experienced as architecture. If you noticed it too often, you were already behind.

Dr. Meredith Halden was waiting in a glass-walled office that looked less designed than resolved.

She rose when Elara entered, though not by much. Halden had the stillness of a woman who understood hierarchy so completely she never needed to perform it at volume. She was older, severe without being harsh, immaculate in the way only people with institutional power ever managed. Her uniform had no visible flaw. Neither, Elara suspected, did her memory.

“Dr. Voss,” Halden said.

“Elara,” Elara replied automatically.

A fractional pause.

Halden’s gaze lifted. “Doctor Voss.”

Elara took the correction without showing she had felt it. “Of course.”

They sat.

The room held almost nothing: two chairs, a pale table, one transparent wall overlooking the vertical interior of the complex. Not calming. Not hostile. Merely designed to discourage the fantasy that comfort was relevant.

Halden touched the surface before her. A projection sheet unfolded between them in cool blue light.

“Your monthly parse summary,” she said.

“I reviewed my performance feed.”

“I’m sure you did. This is the administrative interpretation.”

Elara said nothing.

Halden began to read. “Your diagnostic accuracy in emergent intake pathways has reached ninety-eight point four percent convergence with Aesculon primary outputs.”

The number settled between them.

“In several acute presentation classes,” Halden went on, “particularly cardiothoracic and neurological triage, your independent assessments fall within system-level decision margins.”

She paused.

“This is exceptional.”

The word sounded as if it had been issued under protest.

Elara kept her expression neutral. “Comparable to AI,” she said.

Halden did not answer immediately, and that delay was answer enough.

“Comparable,” she said at last, “within limited, tightly controlled subsets.”

Then, as if the compliment had already exceeded acceptable dosage, she added, “Of course, human variability cannot be normalised across a single cycle.”

Praise, containment, correction. The old administrative rhythm.

Elara inclined her head. “Understood.”

Halden studied her over folded hands. “The concern is not accuracy itself. It is perception.”

“Perception of what?”

“When human outputs approach parity with Aesculon benchmarks,” Halden said, “downstream trust calibration becomes more volatile.”

A polite phrase. A bloodless phrase.

Do not make the system look uncertain.

Elara felt her fingers tighten once against the chair arm. She made them relax. “I’m not sure I follow.”

“You do not need to follow,” Halden said. “You need to be mindful.”

Then, more softly, which made it sharper, “The system is not being evaluated against you, Doctor Voss.”

Her Lifeband pulsed.

Elevated cardiac response.

Halden glanced at the display reflected in the glass. “Increased heart rate,” she said.

“Long shift.”

“Of course.”

No disbelief. No reassurance. Just archival tone.

Halden let the silence work a little longer before she moved on. “There is another matter.”

Elara disliked the sentence on sight.

“The Physician Convergence Trials are entering the next selection cycle,” Halden said.

Elara held still.

She knew the trials. Everyone did. Ten human clinicians, chosen globally, put through a public series of diagnostic and treatment challenges against Aesculon-class systems. They were marketed as celebration, transparency, proof of continuing human value within an optimised medical order. They were also spectacle. Ratings in the billions. Careers made or erased in real time.

“You are on the preliminary consideration list,” Halden said.

“For nomination?”

Halden nodded. “Final selection remains pending. Ten physicians worldwide. Performance threshold is severe.”

Elara said nothing.

“Public engagement is substantial,” Halden continued.

That was a polished way of describing something obscene.

“The last cycle reached global live viewership in the billions. Institutional visibility for participating centres was correspondingly significant.”

Funding. Prestige. preferential access. administrative leverage. She did not need Halden to translate the rest.

“Participation is voluntary,” Halden said.

Then, after just long enough to shape the pressure beneath the sentence: “Visible cooperation, however, tends to produce favourable outcomes for the physicians and institutions involved.”

Elara met her gaze. There it was. Not an invitation. An instrument.

“Would you consent to nomination if selected?” Halden asked.

The office seemed to grow even quieter.

Elara thought of Continuum. Of an ECG begging to be read. Of a machine that had either failed impossibly or lied. Of the fact that she now occupied, however briefly, a space dangerous enough to be noticed.

“I would need to understand the parameters,” she said.

Halden nodded once, as though the answer had been anticipated and already filed. “Naturally. We will proceed under conditional assent unless you submit formal objection.”

Elara did not bother correcting the fiction embedded in the wording.

She rose.

Halden remained seated for a beat, then looked up. “Doctor Voss.”

Elara stopped at the door. “Yes?”

“Be careful,” Halden said.

There was no warmth in it. There was, unexpectedly, something worse than warmth.

Sincerity.

“Exceptional results attract attention,” Halden said. “Not all of it is supportive.”

Elara held her gaze for a fraction of a second too long.

Then she nodded. “Understood.”

She left the office and walked back into the cold, measured light of the Administrative Spine with the distinct and unwelcome feeling that whatever had begun in Continuum was no longer hidden.

Somewhere above her, below her, and all around her, Arc continued its endless work of observation.

For the first time in years, Elara was no longer certain whether she still lived inside a system designed to save lives.

Or inside one that had learned how to decide which lives were worth saving at all.
